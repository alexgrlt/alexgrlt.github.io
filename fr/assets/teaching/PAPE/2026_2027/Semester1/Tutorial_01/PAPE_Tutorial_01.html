Exercise #2

Exercise #2: the question

Franklin et al. (2024), UPSNP

Which parts of the paper by Franklin et al. (2024) on the UPSNP correspond to process evaluation and which to impact evaluation? Which data sources / statistical methods are used? What are their conclusions?

Process evaluation (implementation fidelity):

  • Program design and randomized rollout across woredas.
  • Targeting by community committees, near-universal take-up.
  • Participant profile: mostly older women; ~4% of adults.
  • Outputs: public works hours (12.6% of control), one-for-one crowdout of private work.
  • Data: program records, household surveys, geo-referenced commuting data.

Impact evaluation (causal outcomes):

  • Direct effects: income \(\uparrow\), total hours unchanged.
  • Amenities: significant neighborhood quality gains.
  • Wage spillovers via commuting networks: +14% treated, +3% untreated; +18.6% under full rollout.
  • Welfare analysis: +22.4% with spillovers (6\(\times\) direct effect).
  • Methods: cluster RCT + spatial equilibrium model.

Exercise #2: answer

Process evaluation

\(\rightarrow\) the section “Program and Data”

  • tracks: how the program was implemented, the activities (registration, organization of the works), the outputs (take-up, employment);
  • Data: household survey data;
  • Purely descriptive;
  • Reveals good targeting, excellent take-up (97%).

Impact evaluation

\(\rightarrow\) the rest of the paper

  • Quantifies: impact on employment, amenities, commuting, labor markets;
  • Data: the same household survey;
  • Methods: statistics and regressions based on the RCT;
  • Results: amenities improve, total number of hours worked unchanged; private-sector crowd-out, wages \(\uparrow\).

Both answer different questions

Process evaluation asks was the program delivered as designed?, impact evaluation asks what difference did it make?

Exercise #3

Exercise #3: the question

De Neve et al. (2021), tax compliance

Do De Neve et al. (2021) mostly use qualitative or quantitative evidence? Describe what a mixed method approach could have looked like. How would it have improved the evaluation?

Evidence type:

  • Predominantly quantitative: 4 nationwide RCTs using administrative data. Some about tax filling, some about tax payment + RCTs with/out reminders.
  • Findings: simplification \(>\) deterrence \(\gg\) tax morale.

What a mixed-methods approach could add:

  • Cognitive interviews / focus groups: unpack backfire of tax morale messages.
  • Ethnography in tax offices: observe confusion, barriers to action.
  • User-centered design metrics: letter readability, portal click-throughs, call-center logs.
  • Post-treatment surveys: perceived fairness, trust, deterrence beliefs.

Benefits of mixed methods:

  • Clarify mechanisms, refine message tone/layout.
  • Identify heterogeneous responses (young vs. old, small vs. large liabilities).
  • Enhance external validity and scalability.

Exercise #3: answer

The evidence they use

  • Strictly quantitative: nationwide RCTs on administrative data;
  • Outcome observed: taxes paid (and filing);
  • Nothing on what people did with the letters, or what they thought of them.

What a qualitative arm would add

Focus groups / interviews of Belgian taxpayers, with varying demographics. This could have:

  • given a better sense of the cognitive costs behind the success of the simplified letter;
  • shown how people perceived the deterrence messages;
  • helped explain the counterintuitive results of the social-norms messaging.

Exercise #4: the question

Banerjee et al. (2019), e-governance

Based on Banerjee et al. (2019), how would you do a cost-benefit analysis of the e-governance reform? If you had to compare it with Muralidharan et al. (2016) cited in Banerjee et al. (2019) p.4, what would be your conclusion regarding its cost-effectiveness?

Benefits:

  • Expenditure \(\downarrow\) 24% in Bihar, 19% nationwide (persistent).
  • Fewer ghost households; officials’ asset wealth \(\downarrow\) 10–14%.

Costs:

  • IT infrastructure, training, maintenance.
  • Higher administrative burden for local officials.
  • Payment delays for workers ($+$20 days on average).

CBA steps:

  1. Value fiscal savings (expenditure \(\times\) leakage reduction).
  2. Subtract IT/admin costs.
  3. Monetize worker delay costs (e.g., borrowing rates: but what if people actually need the money to survive?).
  4. Compute NPV, IRR, sensitivity analysis.

Comparison with Smartcards (Muralidharan et al. 2016):

  • Smartcards: increased payments to workers with no extra fiscal cost, faster/safer delivery.
  • CPSMS: strong fiscal savings, modest direct gains, longer delays.

Conclusion:

hard to do as we miss some infos

But a possibility

  • Fiscal efficiency \(\rightarrow\) CPSMS highly cost-effective.
  • Social welfare \(\rightarrow\) Smartcards dominate? (beneficiary gains
    • anti-leakage).

Exercise #4: answer

Compute the costs

  • IT infrastructure (roll-out, maintenance);
  • Staff and training, heavier administrative burden;
  • Delayed payments to beneficiaries \(\rightarrow\) how do we value this?

Quantify the benefits

  • Reduced corruption / leakage;
  • Money saved for the state government budget;
  • Fewer ghost households on the rolls.

Cost-effective… for whom?

From the government’s perspective, the cost-benefit balance is positive and cost-effectiveness is very good. But what happens once we add the large costs borne by beneficiaries? Compare with the smartcards of Muralidharan et al. (2016), which increased payments to workers at no extra fiscal cost. Questions more generally the optimization problem for the state: maximize social welfare… but with which weights? And assuming what kind of preferences?

Appendix

First-Order Stochastic Dominance (FOSD)

Definition

A random variable \(X\) first-order stochastically dominates \(Y\) if

\[F_X(t) \le F_Y(t)\quad \forall\, t\in\mathbb{R},\]

with strict inequality for some \(t\).

  • Intuition: \(X\) yields weakly higher outcomes than \(Y\) at every threshold \(t\);
  • Preference: any decision-maker with increasing utility prefers \(X\) to \(Y\).

Graphical intuition (CDF ordering)

\(F_X\) (dashed) lies everywhere at or below \(F_Y\) (solid) \(\Rightarrow\) \(X\) FOSD \(Y\).

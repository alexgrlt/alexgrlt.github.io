Question 1: type of evaluation

De Neve et al. (2021) evaluate experimentally the effect of nudges to get taxpayers to pay their taxes early. It is:

  1. A Prospective Evaluation.
  2. A Process Evaluation.
  3. A Retrospective Evaluation.

Answer: 1

A prospective evaluation: the evaluation is designed before the letters are sent, together with the intervention (random assignment of letter types, data collection planned in advance).

  • A retrospective evaluation assesses a programme after it has been implemented, using existing data.
  • A process evaluation looks at how the programme is implemented (delivery, compliance with the design), not at its causal effect.

Question 2: survey bias

In Castell et al. (2022), which bias is more likely in the answers to the survey question “Which social benefits have you or a member of your family applied to or received in the last six months?”

  1. Social desirability bias
  2. Recall bias
  3. Anchoring bias

Answer: 2

Recall bias: respondents have to remember every application over six months, for themselves and their family members, and across several benefits.

  • Not anchoring bias: the question suggests no reference value.
  • Social desirability (stigma attached to benefits) could also play a role, but the long recall window (6 months) and reporting for others makes it less important than recall concerns, as stated in the paper (and in the slides of the lecture).

Question 4: omitted variable bias

A research team evaluates the effect of care homes for the elderly on life expectancy. They compare age of death between people who die in care homes and people who die at home. They know that richer people live longer and are more likely to afford care homes. If they do not control for wealth, how will it affect their result?

  1. They will overestimate the benefits of care homes.
  2. They will underestimate the benefits of care homes.
  3. They will underestimate the costs of care homes.

Answer: 1

Omitted variable bias = (effect of wealth on age of death) × (correlation between wealth and care homes) \(= (+) \times (+) > 0\).

People in care homes are richer, and would have lived longer anyway: part of the gap in age of death is due to wealth, not to care homes. The estimated benefit of care homes is too large.

Reminder: imagine we have the true model

\[Y = \alpha + \tau X_1 + \gamma X_2 + \epsilon\]

but we estimate

\[Y = \alpha + \beta X_1 + \nu\]

You know that the estimated coefficient will be \(\hat\beta = \frac{Cov(X_1,Y)}{V(X_1)}\), replace with the true model for \(Y\):

\[\hat\beta = = \frac{Cov(X_1,\alpha + \tau X_1 + \gamma X_2 + \epsilon)}{V(X_1)} = \tau + \frac{Cov(X_1, \gamma X_2)}{V(X_1)} = \tau + \gamma \frac{Cov(X_1, X_2)}{V(X_1)}\]

So the bias is \(\gamma \frac{Cov(X_1, X_2)}{V(X_1)}\) which is the coefficient of \(X_2\) in the true model times the coefficient of \(X_1\) in the regression of \(X_2\) on \(X_1\).

Question 6: ATE vs. ATT

In which situation will the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) differ from the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT)?

  1. When selection into treatment is dependent on potential outcomes
  2. When treatment effects are heterogeneous
  3. When selection into treatment is dependent on potential outcomes and effects are heterogeneous

Answer: 3

Write \(p = P(D = 1)\) and the treatment effect for the untreated \(\text{ATU} = \E(Y_1 - Y_0 \mid D = 0)\). Then \[ \text{ATE} = p \cdot \text{ATT} + (1 - p) \cdot \text{ATU} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{ATE} - \text{ATT} = (1 - p)\,(\text{ATU} - \text{ATT}). \] ATE \(\neq\) ATT only if the treated and the untreated have different average gains. Both conditions are needed:

  • Heterogeneity without selection: the treated are representative of the whole sample, so their average gain is the same as the one untreated would get (ATU = ATE). Option 2 alone is not enough.
  • Selection without heterogeneity: \(\forall x \left(\text{"no matter characteristic } x \text{"}\right),\ \E(Y_1 - Y_0 \mid X = x) = \E(Y_1 - Y_0) = \delta\). In particular this holds when \(X\) is the treatment status, so ATT = ATU = ATE \(= \delta\). Option 1 alone is not enough.

Rubin causal model

Setup and notation

Imagine a public policy. For an individual \(i\), \(T_i = 1\) if \(i\) receives the policy, \(T_i = 0\) if not.

Say the policy is access to an AI app to learn how to read in first grade (one of my research projects). We denote:

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_i(1) &: \text{the result of the student if she is taught with the AI app} \\ Y_i(0) &: \text{the result of the student if she is NOT taught with the AI app} \end{aligned} \]

This is the Rubin potential outcomes model: for each scenario, we posit that there is a fixed score that the student would obtain under that scenario.

Treatment effects

The question is then: what would happen if we switched from one scenario to the other?

\[ \tau = \E\big(Y(1) - Y(0)\big) \]

Because the “change of scenario” is keen to a new treatment of the individual, we call this the average treatment effect (ATE).

Sometimes it can only be estimated for the group of treated people, \(\E\big(Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T = 1\big)\): this is the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT).

Unconfoundedness

Unconfoundedness

Also known as conditional independence, ignorability or selection on observables.

Unconfoundedness means that, among individuals with the same characteristics, looking at their treatment status tells us nothing about their potential outcomes.

Formally, the potential outcomes of an individual are independent of their treatment status once we condition on their observable characteristics:

\[ \big(Y_i(1),\, Y_i(0)\big) \independent T_i \mid X_i \]

Assessing unconfoundedness: placebo checks

Unconfoundedness is not directly testable: it is a statement about untreated potential outcome \(Y_i(0)\) for the treated, which we never observe.

We can however test a proxy: find variables for which we know the true effect is zero, and run the same estimator (same covariates \(X\), same matching). If it still finds an “effect”, the treated and control groups differ in something that \(X\) does not capture.

Placebo outcome

An outcome the treatment cannot affect, typically because it was determined before the intervention.

Example

Imagine you are evaluating the effectiveness of renovating schools. We estimate its effect on the end-of-year math score by regression (or matching) on \(X\): priority-education status of the school, class size, teacher experience, student gender and month of birth.

Placebo: pick an outcome renovation should not change. Same regression, but with student SES (e.g. parents’ occupation) as the outcome: \[ \text{SES}_i = \alpha + \tau^{\text{placebo}}\, T_i + X_i'\beta + u_i \]

Reading the placebo results (fake numbers here)

  • Scenario A: the estimator finds nothing where there is nothing to find. Consistent with unconfoundedness, so the 0.20 s.d. effect is more credible.
  • Scenario B: even after controlling for \(X\), renovated schools have richer students who were already better in math in September. Part of the 0.20 is likely selection.

Why only suggestive?

  • Passing the proxy test does not prove unconfoundedness. The placebos only check selection on SES, baseline scores, etc. Something else could still drive both renovation and math progress without showing up in SES.
  • Failing is more informative: the estimator does not even balance the groups on things we can see, so we should doubt it on things we cannot.
  • The placebo outcome must be truly unaffected: measured before the treatment, or a (very) plausibly unaffected variable.

Overlap and functional form

Overlap: when we need it

Overlap (common support): for every value \(x\) of the covariates, we can find both treated and control individuals: \[ 0 < P(D = 1 \mid X = x) < 1 \quad \text{for all } x. \]

Under unconfoundedness, the ATE is a comparison at each value of \(X\) (following the law of iterated expectation): \[ \tau = \E_X\Big[\underbrace{\E[Y \mid D = 1, X]}_{\text{needs treated units at } X} - \underbrace{\E[Y \mid D = 0, X]}_{\text{needs control units at } X}\Big]. \]

What if overlap fails?

When we don’t have overlap, there are some values \(x\) of \(X\) where there are no control units (resp. treatment units), the second (resp. first) term is never observed: the data are silent about some counterfactuals.

The way linear regression works is by extrapolation: learn \(\E[Y \mid D = 0, X]\) where controls exist, and assume it keeps the same shape where they do not. This is the functional form assumption implicit in linear regression.

The functional form assumption

We assume that the outcome without treatment follows a known parametric shape (typically linear in \(X\)), and that this shape also holds outside the range of \(X\) where we can observe it: \[ \E[Y(0) \mid X = x] = \alpha + \beta x \quad \text{for all } x, \text{ including those where there is no control unit.} \]

  • With overlap, the same model only interpolates between observed points: we can check it against the data, and a mistake is small.
  • Without overlap, it extrapolates: nothing in the data can confirm or reject the assumption. The estimate rests on the model, not on the data.

No overlap: linear truth vs. non-linear truth

  • Left: the linear shape really does continue beyond the controls, so the extrapolated line is a good counterfactual.
  • Right: the true \(Y(0)\) flattens out, but the data on controls cannot tell us so: the line overshoots, and the estimated ATE is biased.

What can we do?

  • Look at overlap first: histograms of \(X\) by treatment group, or of the estimated propensity score \(\hat P(D = 1 \mid X)\).
  • Restrict to the common support (trimming, matching): we then estimate the effect only where we have both groups, and say so: it is a different estimand from the ATE of the whole population.

Linear regression

Estimating the treatment effect

  • Goal: understand how an outcome \(Y\) varies with explanatory variables \(X\).
  • Naively: look at \(\Cov(Y, X)\), or at mean differences of \(Y\) across values of \(X\)…

…this is long and tedious.

  • What we most often want is the expected value of \(Y\) given \(X\):

\[ \E[Y \mid X] \equiv \text{Conditional Expectation Function (CEF)}. \]

The role of the regression is to find the best linear approximation to the CEF.

Why regression?

  • The CEF \(m(x)\) can be arbitrarily complex and non-linear.
  • Regression offers a parametric approximation: \(m(x) \approx \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\).
  • OLS finds the \(\beta_0, \beta_1\) that minimise \(\E\big[(Y - \beta_0 - \beta_1 X)^2\big]\).
  • OLS gives the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) to the true CEF: \[ \E[Y \mid X] \approx \E[Y] + \beta_1\big(X - \E[X]\big). \]

In practice, with one covariate: \[ \beta_1 = \frac{\Cov(Y, X)}{\Var(X)}. \]

Causality

Let \(Y(d)\) denote potential outcomes under treatment level \(d\). The “causal CEF” (do not use this term, it just conveys the idea) is \[ \E[Y(d)] \quad \text{or} \quad \E[Y(d) \mid X]. \]

  • But we only observe \(Y = D\,Y(1) + (1 - D)\,Y(0)\).
  • We want \(\E[Y(1) - Y(0)]\) (ATE).
  • To link the observed CEF \(\E[Y \mid D, X]\) to the causal one, we need \[ \big(Y(1), Y(0)\big) \independent D \mid X \qquad \text{(unconfoundedness / ignorability).} \]

Under ignorability

  • \(\E[Y \mid D = 1, X] = \E[Y(1) \mid X]\) and \(\E[Y \mid D = 0, X] = \E[Y(0) \mid X]\).
  • Therefore: \[ \E[Y(1) - Y(0)] = \E_X\Big[\E[Y \mid D = 1, X] - \E[Y \mid D = 0, X]\Big]. \]
  • The conditional expectation function of \(Y\) on \((D, X)\) encodes causal effects when the identification assumptions hold.

OLS as an estimator of the CEF

Regression model: \[ Y_i = \alpha + \tau D_i + X_i^\top \beta + u_i, \qquad \E[u_i \mid D_i, X_i] = 0. \]

Interpretation

  • The regression function \(\E[Y \mid D, X] = \alpha + \tau D + X'\beta\) is an approximation of the true CEF.
  • If the model is correctly specified and uncounfoundedness holds, \(\tau\) identifies the causal effect of \(D\).
  • Otherwise, it gives the best linear approximation to \(\E[Y \mid D, X]\).

Regression with a randomised treatment

\[ Y_i = \alpha + \tau D_i + u_i, \qquad D_i \independent u_i. \]

  • Randomisation ensures \(\E[u_i \mid D_i] = 0\).
  • Thus \(\E[Y \mid D] = \alpha + \tau D\) is the true CEF.
  • \(\Rightarrow\) OLS recovers the causal mean difference: \[ \tau = \E[Y \mid D = 1] - \E[Y \mid D = 0] = \E[Y(1) - Y(0)]. \]

Standard error of estimates

With a single covariate \(X\), the variance of the estimated coefficient is \[ V(\hat\beta) = \frac{V(\text{residual})}{(n - 1)\cdot s^2_X}. \]

With several covariates, \(X_j\) is partly explained by the others. The coefficient \(\beta_j\) captures the effect of the part of \(X_j\) that is not correlated with the other \(X_{m \neq j}\) (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem). What matters is the variation of that unexplained part, not the full variation:

\[ V(\hat\beta_j) = \frac{V(\text{residual})}{(1 - R_j^2)\cdot \sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{ji} - \bar x_j)^2} = \frac{V(\text{residual})}{(1 - R_j^2)\cdot (n - 1)\cdot s^2_{X_j}} \]

Homoskedasticity

Why should we care about the variance of the error?

“When residuals are homoskedastic, we use the constant variance \(\sigma^2\) to estimate the variance of the coefficients. But how can the variance of the residuals be constant at all? It is never perfectly constant in a sample, so which value do we take?”

Remind: \[ V(\hat\beta) = \frac{\color{#d90502}{V(\text{residual})}}{(n - 1)\cdot s^2_X} \]

Thus, the variance of \(\hat\beta\) is a function of the variance of the error term (here approximated by the sample variance of the residuals).

What is random? The frequentist view

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i \]

  • \(\beta_0, \beta_1\): fixed but unknown numbers (population parameters). In frequentist statistics, they are not random.
  • \(x_i\): treated as given (we reason conditionally on \(X\)). Thus, it’s NOT the source of uncertainty / randomness.
  • \(u_i\), the error: everything else that moves \(y_i\) (omitted factors, luck, measurement error). This is the random part.

A simple thought experiment

Imagine drawing the sample again and again. Each time, the \(u_i\) come out differently \(\Rightarrow\) the \(y_i\) differ \(\Rightarrow\) \(\hat\beta\) differs. All the sampling variability of \(\hat\beta\) comes from \(u\): the variance of \(\hat\beta\) inherits the variance of \(u\).

sidenote: why “the frequentist view”?

2 main approaches to statistics

  • Frequentist (dominant): \(\beta\)s are fixed, data are random \(\Rightarrow\) uncertainty = sampling variability (comes from the error term).

  • Bayesian (less common): data are fixed, \(\beta\)s described by a probability distribution \(\Rightarrow\) uncertainty = posterior distribution.

In practice, main difference is about the question asked: “How would \(\hat\beta\) vary across samples? (due to randomness of the data draw)” vs. “What do I believe about \(\beta\) given this sample? (given my prior beliefs and the new data I have)”

Distribution of the error term

We cannot know each \(u_i\), but we can make assumptions about the distribution it is drawn from. The classical one: \[ u_i \mid X_i \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(0,\ \sigma^2) \]

  • Mean 0: given \(X\), the unobserved factors do not push \(y\) up or down on average (\(\E[u \mid X] = 0\), exogeneity).
  • Variance \(\sigma^2\): how spread out the draws are, i.e. the width of the bell curve. This is the variance we are talking about.
  • Homoskedasticity: this width is the same whatever the value of \(X\): \[ \Var(u_i \mid X_i = x) = \sigma^2 \quad \text{for all } x. \qquad \text{Heteroskedasticity: } \Var(u_i \mid X_i = x) = \sigma^2(x). \]

So “constant variance” describes the distribution each error is drawn from, not the numbers in our sample: the realised \(u_i\) are all different.

The picture: one bell curve at each value of \(X\)

Why do we need an assumption on the variance?

  • For each individual we observe one \(y_i\), hence one draw of \(u_i\) (and only through \(\hat u_i\)). One draw cannot reveal the variance of the distribution it came from.
  • Homoskedasticity reduces this to one unknown, \(\sigma^2\), which we can estimate by pooling all the residuals.

Alternatives to a constant variance

  • Parametric: assume a shape for \(\sigma^2(x)\), e.g. \(\sigma^2(x) = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 x\): a few more parameters to estimate (weighted / feasible GLS). See e.g. Boyd et al. (2013)
  • Non-parametric: either estimate \(\sigma^2(x)\) by smoothing \(\hat u_i^2\) against \(x\) (needs a lot of data), or use robust standard errors, which never need each \(\sigma_i^2\) separately and are valid in large samples.

The variance covariance matrix

The regression involves the whole vector of error terms for all individuals \(u = (u_1, \dots, u_n)'\). We can put in the matrix the variance of the error-term for each unit as well as the covariances between errors terms: \[ \Var(u \mid X) = \begin{pmatrix} \Var(u_1 \mid X) & \Cov(u_1, u_2 \mid X) & \cdots & \Cov(u_1, u_n \mid X) \\ \Cov(u_2, u_1 \mid X) & \Var(u_2 \mid X) & \cdots & \Cov(u_2, u_n \mid X) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \Cov(u_n, u_1 \mid X) & \Cov(u_n, u_2 \mid X) & \cdots & \Var(u_n \mid X) \end{pmatrix} = \Omega \]

  • Diagonal: the width of each individual’s bell curve, \(\sigma_i^2\). Homoskedasticity \(\Rightarrow\) all equal to \(\sigma^2\).
  • Off-diagonal: do the draws of two individuals move together? With independent draws (e.g. random sampling), they are \(0\).

Three possible assumptions about \(\Omega\) (illustration with \(n = 4\))

Homoskedastic, uncorrelated

\[ \Omega = \sigma^2 I_n = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma^2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma^2 \end{pmatrix} \]

Same bell curve for every individual, independent draws. One unknown parameter.

Heteroskedastic, uncorrelated

\[ \Omega = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2^2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma_3^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma_4^2 \end{pmatrix} \]

Width differs across individuals (often with \(X_i\)), draws still independent.

Correlated (clustered) errors

\[ \Omega = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \sigma_{12} & 0 & 0 \\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_2^2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma_3^2 & \sigma_{34} \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma_{34} & \sigma_4^2 \end{pmatrix} \]

Draws are correlated within groups (villages, classrooms), e.g. individuals 1–2 and 3–4.

In all three cases \(\hat\beta\) is unbiased (this only needs \(\E[u \mid X] = 0\)). What changes is its variance: \[ \Var(\hat\beta \mid X) = (X'X)^{-1}\, X'\Omega X\, (X'X)^{-1} \]

Interpreting regression coefficients

Interpreting regression coefficients

\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3\, x_1 \times x_2 + u \]

with \(y\) log monthly earnings, \(x_1\) a dummy for being female, \(x_2\) a dummy for having gone to college.

  • \(\beta_0\): mean log earnings of men without a college degree (the omitted category).
  • \(\beta_1 \times 100\): female–male gap (in %) among people without a college degree.
  • \(\beta_2 \times 100\): gap (in %) between male college graduates and men without a degree.
  • \(\beta_3 \times 100\): how much the female–male gap changes for college graduates (the gap among graduates is \(\beta_1 + \beta_3\)).

The overall effect of a college degree for women is thus \(\beta_2 + \beta_3\).

Heterogeneous effects in a regression

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 T + \beta_2 x_i + \beta_3\, T \times x_i + u_i \]

with \(y\) the outcome, \(T\) a treatment dummy, \(x_i\) an individual characteristic, say a dummy for individual \(i\) being female.

  • \(\beta_1\) is the ATE for the subpopulation with \(x_i = 0\): here, the average treatment effect for men.
  • \(\beta_3\) is the difference in the treatment effect between women and men.
  • If the treatment effect is homogeneous, \(\beta_3 = 0\) and \(\beta_1\) is the ATE for the full sample.

Omitted variable bias

Omitted variable bias: setup

Suppose the true model is \[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i} + \epsilon_i \]

but we only observe (or only include in the regression) \(X_1\), not \(X_2\). We estimate instead \[ Y_i = \tilde\beta_0 + \tilde\beta_1 X_{1i} + \tilde\epsilon_i \]

Omitted variable bias: formula

In this regression, the coefficient \(\tilde\beta_1\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde\beta_1 &= \frac{\Cov(X_1, Y)}{\Var(X_1)} = \frac{\Cov\big(X_1,\ \overbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \epsilon}^{\text{true model for } Y}\big)}{\Var(X_1)} \\[0.5em] &= \frac{\beta_1 \Cov(X_1, X_1) + \beta_2 \Cov(X_1, X_2) + \Cov(X_1, \epsilon)}{\Var(X_1)} = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \cdot \frac{\Cov(X_1, X_2)}{\Var(X_1)} \end{aligned} \]

and \(\dfrac{\Cov(X_1, X_2)}{\Var(X_1)}\) is the coefficient \(\delta_1\) in the regression \[ X_2 = \alpha + \delta_1 X_1 + u. \]

Omitted variables and conditional independence

“Would it also be correct to say that conditional independence ensures that, for every value of \(x\), the omitted variables captured in the error term have no effect on \(y\)?”

The conditional independence assumption is about the average of the residuals: \[ \E(u \mid X) = 0. \]

  • The covariates in the regression must not be related to the error term: we cannot predict the error term from any of the covariates.
  • Unobserved covariates may matter for predicting \(Y\): if they are independent of the included \(X\), the assumption still holds. That does not mean they have no effect on \(y\).
  • The assumption only ensures that the coefficients on \(X\) are unbiased, not that the prediction model is the best one. And it is an assumption: you cannot rely on it blindly.

OVB in R: omitted variable unrelated to \(x_1\)

Here x2 affects y, but is independent of x1: dropping it does not bias the coefficient on x1 (true value: 2).

set.seed(42)
n  <- 1000
x1 <- rnorm(n)
x2 <- rnorm(n)                         # independent of x1
y  <- 1 + 2 * x1 + 3 * x2 + rnorm(n)

model1 <- lm(y ~ x1)                   # only x1
model2 <- lm(y ~ x1 + x2)              # x1 and x2
rbind(only_x1 = coef(model1)["x1"], x1_and_x2 = coef(model2)["x1"])
                x1
only_x1   2.007476
x1_and_x2 1.978201

OVB in R: omitted variable correlated with \(x_1\)

Now x2 is correlated with x1: omitting it biases the coefficient on x1, by \(\beta_2 \cdot \delta_1 = 3 \times 0.8 = 2.4\).

set.seed(42)
n  <- 1000
x1 <- rnorm(n)
x2 <- 0.8 * x1 + rnorm(n)              # correlated with x1
y  <- 1 + 2 * x1 + 3 * x2 + rnorm(n)

model1 <- lm(y ~ x1)                   # only x1
model2 <- lm(y ~ x1 + x2)              # x1 and x2
rbind(only_x1 = coef(model1)["x1"], x1_and_x2 = coef(model2)["x1"])
                x1
only_x1   4.407476
x1_and_x2 1.992831

Probit

Binary dependent variables

Suppose you want to predict the probability that a variable \(Y\) equals one (e.g. whether a person will be employed next month).

  1. Linear probability model: assume the probability is linear in \(X\) and run a linear regression.
  2. Latent variable model: assume the probability depends on whether individuals cross some threshold.

(Other, non-parametric methods are not covered here.)

The latent variable model

\[ P(Y = 1 \mid X) = P(Y^\star > 0 \mid X) \]

Suppose \(Y^\star = X\beta + \epsilon\). Then

\[ P(Y = 1 \mid X) = P(X\beta + \epsilon > 0 \mid X) = P(\epsilon > -X\beta \mid X) \] Assume \(\epsilon\) follows a symmetric distribution with respect to 0 (eg. the standard normal, or the logistic with mean 0):

\[ P(Y = 1 \mid X) = P(\epsilon > -X\beta \mid X) = P(\epsilon < X\beta \mid X) \]

Once we have assumed a function form for the distribution of the disturbance \(\epsilon\), we can just use maximum likelihood to recover the \(\beta\)s.

The name of the method depends on the assumption on \(\epsilon\):

  • normal distribution \(\Rightarrow\) probit;
  • logistic distribution \(\Rightarrow\) logit.

The probit link

In the probit case, \(P(\epsilon < X\beta \mid X) = \Phi(X\beta)\), the standard normal CDF:

Marginal effects

When we say regression coefficients are effects “holding other characteristics constant”, we mean they are marginal effects: \[ \frac{\partial Y}{\partial x_1} = \frac{\partial\,(\alpha + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \dots)}{\partial x_1} = \beta_1 \]

When the model is highly non-linear, things are not that simple. In the probit case: \[ \frac{\partial P(Y = 1 \mid X)}{\partial x_1} = \frac{\partial\, G(X\beta)}{\partial x_1} = \beta_1 \cdot g(X\beta) \]

A small change \(\Delta x_1\) thus has an effect \(\Delta x_1 \cdot \beta_1 \cdot g(X\beta)\), where \(g(\cdot)\) is the density of the normal distribution: the effect depends on \(X\).

Variance of a sum

Intuition

  • If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, the way \(X\) fluctuates tells us nothing about \(Y\).
  • Their ups and downs do not systematically cancel out or reinforce each other.
  • Hence the total spread (variance) is simply the sum: \[ \Var(X \pm Y) = \Var(X) + \Var(Y). \]

Formal decomposition

For any \(X\), \(Y\) and constants \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\): \[ \Var(aX + bY) = a^2 \Var(X) + b^2 \Var(Y) + 2ab \Cov(X, Y). \]

Special case (difference): \[ \Var(X - Y) = \Var(X) + \Var(Y) - 2 \Cov(X, Y). \]

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(\Cov(X, Y) = 0\), so \[ \Var(X - Y) = \Var(X) + \Var(Y). \]

Type I error and significance

Significance level and “statistical significance”

“The slides define the significance level as \(\alpha\), the probability of rejecting the null when it is true (Type I error). But they also say that statistical significance is ‘how unlikely it is that we commit a Type I error’, i.e. \(1 - \alpha\). Which is it?”

Both, because they are complements. Fix the state of the world: \(H_0\) is true. Then our test can only do one of two things, which are mutually exclusive: \[ \underbrace{P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true})}_{\alpha\;=\;\text{Type I error}} \;+\; \underbrace{P(\text{do not reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true})}_{1 - \alpha\;=\;\text{no Type I error}} \;=\; 1 \]

The four possible outcomes of a test

\(H_0\) true \(H_0\) false (\(H_1\) true)
Reject \(H_0\) Type I error (false positive)
probability \(\alpha\)		 Correct decision
probability \(1 - \beta\)
Do not reject \(H_0\) Correct decision
probability \(1 - \alpha\) (confidence level)		 Type II error (false negative)
probability \(\beta\)
Column total \(\alpha + (1 - \alpha) = 1\) \(\beta + (1 - \beta) = 1\)

Complementarity holds within a column (same state of the world), never across columns: \(\alpha + \beta \neq 1\).

Which of these is statistical power?

Statistical power

\[\text{Power } = P\left(\text{Reject the null}\mid \text{The null is false}\right)\] Moreover, remind that:

\[\text{Reject the null} \Leftrightarrow |t|\geq1.96\] Hence:

\[\text{Power } = P\left(|t|\geq1.96\mid \tau \neq 0\right)\]

Consequence:

  • Since power depends on \(t = \frac{\hat\tau}{se}\):
    • it depends on sample size (because the larger the sample size, the smaller the standard error).
    • It also depends on the covariates in the regression, because they lower the error.
    • It relies on the structure of the errors: heteroskedastic errors imply the standard error is larger. Same for clustered errors.
  • Power depends on the actual effect \(\tau\): the larger \(\tau\), the more likely \(\hat\tau\) is far from 0.
